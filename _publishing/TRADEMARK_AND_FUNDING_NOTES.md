## 1. HAIMU and "Human-AI Mutual Understandability" — Trademark

### Should you trademark?

**Reasons to consider it**
- You originated the methodology and use it in public (haimu.world, UniWorld, future projects). A registered mark gives you a clear claim in your jurisdiction(s) and makes licensing to businesses or partners explicit and defensible.
- If Grand Beta or other entities will offer "HAIMU-based" consulting or training, a trademark (and a simple license from you) clarifies that they are authorized users and reduces confusion with third parties using the term without permission.
- Registration is relatively low cost compared to the value of a defined methodology brand.

**What’s involved (high level)**

- **US (USPTO)**  
  - File an application specifying classes of goods/services (e.g. education, consulting, software development methodology).  
  - Fee: about **$350 per class** (more for multiple classes).  
  - Process: examination, possible office actions, then registration. Timeline often 8–12+ months.  
  - You can file as an individual; the mark can be licensed to companies later.

- **Canada (CIPO)**  
  - File through CIPO’s online system.  
  - Fee: about **$478 CAD** first class, **~$145** per additional class (check current fees).  
  - Similar examination process; timelines vary.

- **Strategy**  
  - Many methodology creators register in their home country first (Canada if you’re there, or US if that’s your primary market), then add the other later if needed.  
  - Working with a trademark lawyer for the first filing is recommended (clear class selection, description of services, and avoiding refusals).

### Licensing to businesses or partners

- You (as owner) grant a **license** to Grand Beta or others to use "HAIMU" and/or "Human-AI Mutual Understandability" in connection with specified services (e.g. consulting, training).  
- License can be **non-exclusive** (you can license others) or **exclusive** for certain fields/regions.  
- Put it in writing (short license agreement): who can use the mark, for what, where, and whether they must use it as "HAIMU (methodology by Sean MacNutt)" or similar to preserve attribution.  
- You do not need to have a registered trademark to grant a license, but registration strengthens your position and the value of the license.

### Fair use (you don’t control these)

- **Nominative fair use**  
  Others may use your mark to **refer to** you or your methodology (e.g. “trained in HAIMU,” “uses the HAIMU methodology by Sean MacNutt”) when it’s necessary to identify the thing and there’s no suggestion of sponsorship or endorsement.  
  So: **public discussion, press, academic papers, reviews, and criticism** that refer to HAIMU as your methodology are generally fair use — you cannot stop people from talking about or citing it.  
  You can still object if someone uses the mark in a way that suggests they *are* you or officially endorsed (e.g. “Official HAIMU Certification” without your permission).

- **Descriptive / generic use**  
  If "Human-AI Mutual Understandability" were used only in a generic, descriptive sense (“we do human-AI mutual understandability”) and not as a brand for your specific methodology, that could be a different type of use. In practice, keeping "HAIMU" and "Human-AI Mutual Understandability" tied to your methodology and haimu.world helps maintain them as a brand.

- **Academic and press**  
  Citing the methodology, discussing it, or reporting on it is normal referential use. You can encourage proper attribution (e.g. “HAIMU™ by Sean MacNutt” or “see haimu.world”) without restricting fair use.

**Summary**  
Registering HAIMU (and optionally "Human-AI Mutual Understandability") in at least one jurisdiction is reasonable for a methodology you’re commercializing and licensing. Plan for a few hundred dollars and 8–12+ months; use a lawyer for the application. License to businesses in writing; accept that press, academic, and referential use remain fair use.

## 2. JANL (janl.world) — Concept, URL, and Trademark

### What you described

- **JANL**: Originally “JSON-syntax Algorithmic Natural Language”; also known as “Just Another Notation Language” (including from an AI “hallucination”).  
- **Idea**: KVPs and nested structures for instructions and robust training methods for AI — a refined, structured format for AI instruction and protocols, distinct from ad-hoc JSON or bicolumn pedagogy.  
- **Plans**: Train models to process this format as pedagogy (structured, protocol-driven) rather than only bicolumn formats.

### Value of the concept

- **Differentiation**  
  “People use JSON” is broad; a *named*, *specified* format (syntax + semantics + use cases for AI instruction and training) is a product. If JANL is a clear schema + best practices + pedagogy (e.g. how to represent instructions, nesting, tool use), it can sit alongside JSON as “JANL: JSON-syntax algorithmic natural language for AI instruction and training.”

- **Pedagogy and tooling**  
  If models are trained to *expect* and *produce* JANL, it becomes a teachable protocol (like “write in JANL” prompts). That has value for tooling, reproducibility, and instruction design.

- **“Been thought of”**  
  Many people use JSON for configs and APIs; some use structured formats for AI prompts or agent protocols. What’s less common is a **named, documented, pedagogy-oriented** format with a clear identity (JANL) and a plan to train models on it. So the concept can still deserve a name and a home.

### Should you get janl.world?

- **If you intend to** publish a spec, examples, and/or tooling and to use “JANL” as a public name for this approach, **yes** — securing janl.world is low cost and avoids someone else taking the name. It doesn’t commit you to a full product immediately; you can use it as a landing page and later add spec, repo, and pedagogy materials.

- **If JANL is only internal or experimental** for now, you could still register the domain to reserve the name and point it at a “Coming soon” or a link to aguywithai.world/projects/janl (or similar) until you’re ready.

### Trademark for JANL

- **Later decision**  
  Trademark when you have (or are about to have) real use in commerce: a public spec, tools, or training materials offered under the name JANL.  
  Until then, the **domain (janl.world)** is enough to stake the name and build the brand. You can add a trademark (e.g. for “software; specification of a data format; education services”) when you launch.

**Summary**  
The concept has value as a named, structured approach to AI instruction and training. Securing **janl.world** is recommended if you plan to use “JANL” publicly. Trademark can follow once you have concrete use (spec, tools, or pedagogy).

## 3. Crowdfunded Seed Investment — Tools and Limits

### Kickstarter and equity

- **Kickstarter does not allow equity or shares.**  
  Backers get rewards (product, perks, etc.), not ownership. The platform has explicitly stayed out of equity crowdfunding. So you **cannot** use Kickstarter to “distribute shares” or run a seed round.

### Equity crowdfunding (shares / SAFEs)

- **US — Regulation Crowdfunding (Reg CF)**  
  - Companies raise **up to $5 million/year** through **SEC-registered funding portals** (or broker-dealers).  
  - You **cannot** run the offer on your own site only; it must go through a registered portal.  
  - **Platforms**: Wefunder, StartEngine, Republic, DealMaker, etc.  
  - **Typical cost**: ~7–8% of the amount raised at close (e.g. Wefunder ~7.9%) plus sometimes an annual admin fee; no upfront fee at Wefunder if you don’t close.  
  - Investors can be non-accredited; there are per-investor limits.  
  - Securities are restricted (e.g. one-year hold); many offers use **SAFEs** (Simple Agreements for Future Equity) instead of priced equity.

- **Canada — Start-up Crowdfunding (NI 45-110)**  
  - **Up to ~$1.5 million** in 12 months through a **registered or exempt funding portal**.  
  - Portals must be compliant with Canadian securities rules (e.g. equitycrowd.fund, SMV Capital Markets, and others).  
  - Per-investor limits (e.g. $2,500 per offering, or higher with advice).  
  - Company must meet eligibility (e.g. head office in Canada, operating business).

### “Tools that sell your shares”

- **What exists**  
  The **platforms themselves** are the tools: you create an offering (amount, instrument — e.g. SAFE or equity), set terms, and the platform hosts the raise, collects funds, and often helps with cap table / investor management. You don’t “load shares” onto a generic marketplace; you run a **regulated offering** on one of these portals.

- **Building your own**  
  To sell equity (or SAFEs) to the public, you must use a **registered portal** (US) or compliant portal (Canada). Building your own “equity selling” site would require becoming a registered broker-dealer or funding portal (significant cost and compliance). So in practice you **use an existing platform**, not build one.

### Partial seed round via crowdfunding

- You can do a **hybrid**: part of the seed from angels/VCs and part from **equity crowdfunding** (Reg CF in US or NI 45-110 in Canada).  
- The crowdfunding portion is capped (e.g. $5M US, ~$1.5M Canada per year) and has disclosure and ongoing obligations.  
- Using a platform (e.g. Wefunder in US, or a Canadian portal) is the normal path; they handle investor onboarding, payments, and compliance structure.

**Summary**  
- **Kickstarter**: Rewards only; no shares.  
- **Equity seed round**: Use a **registered funding portal** (US: Wefunder, StartEngine, etc.; Canada: NI 45-110 portals). You don’t “load shares” into a generic tool; you run a compliant offering on the platform.  
- Building your own equity-distribution tool would mean becoming a regulated entity; not practical for a single raise.  
- Partial crowdfunded seed is possible: run a Reg CF (or Canadian) round alongside traditional seed investors.
